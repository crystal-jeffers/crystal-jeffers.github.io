What I bring to the table

When I build systems, the felt change is an everyday experience of ease when taking action, searching for key details, and tracking progress. It's less talk, more movement, and faster coordination for the whole team. Here are a few examples of how that shows up in real time.

  1. New hires
    become self-sufficient sooner.

    Guides and checklists introduce workflows through a natural progression with intuitive scaffolding, so new hires build confidence on their own instead of pulling experienced teammates off the job for every question.

  2. The team
    doesn't depend on who remembers.

    The process lives in the system, not in one person's head, so a vacation, a promotion, or a departure doesn't stall jobs or customer commitments.

  3. Leaders
    stop fielding every question.

    Status, next steps, and answers reach people before they have to ask, so managers spend their time on decisions, customers, and growth instead of chasing updates.

  4. The department
    adapts to change without falling behind.

    When a platform or process shifts, the documentation and tools shift with it, so the team keeps its footing through rollouts, departures, and additions.

  5. Over time
    systems keep getting better.

    I don't hand off and walk away. Each system is watched in use and refined from what people actually do with it, so it improves as the team grows.

  6. The result

    Departments can take on more work without being outpaced by coordination load.

How I work

The same five steps sit behind every project.

  1. Listen for friction

    Sometimes things jump out at me, sometimes they're brought to my attention through an indirect comment or an explicit request for help. The signals I listen for:

    • The same question asked twice
    • A side spreadsheet kept "just in case"
    • "Who has the latest version?"
    • Information re-typed from one place to another
    • A task that only works when one person is in

    In practice

    • After I showed another dispatcher my first draft of the training guide, she said, "I wish there were something like this when I started!"Full Details
    • The Service Manager pulled out 12 pages of paper SOPs with dense paragraphs of text and said, "Our techs don't really read this."Full Details
    • The Airtable manager dashboard started with a request from the Service Manager to "freshen up" the old New Hire Protocols.Full Details
  2. Spot the real problem

    When something slips, I look at the system before the person. I identify bottlenecks, redundancies, and communication breakdowns, then ask myself, "What would make the right action the easy one?"

    In practice

    • When a technician flagged incorrect PM parts, I built a monthly audit so each technician sees, and can correct, the parts to be ordered for their sites that month.Full Details
    • When delayed POs were slowing job closure, I developed a way to request consolidated invoices from vendors, instead of waiting for system flags and in-the-moment delays to signal the need.Full Details
  3. Determine use cases and ownership

    I look at the end goal, the most efficient way to get there, and decide up front which decisions stay with whom. I simplify information, filter for relevance, and streamline steps for efficiency.

    In practice

    • The original New Hire Protocols named owners for some tasks, but gave no broader view of who is responsible for what. In the new system, every step has one owner, from request to confirmation.Full Details
    • The original start-up procedures bundled every role's responsibilities into one document. The mobile site gives each role its own view, so people find the right information faster.Full Details
    • Union holiday conflicts are flagged on the dispatch board, never blocked. The supervisor keeps the final call.Full Details
  4. Design intuitive solutions with user-friendly interfaces

    I consider existing tools and habits, identify potential break points, and develop tools that improve access without adding complexity.

    In practice

    • I'm comfortable with VBA and macros, but most people aren't, even when it's just pressing a button. Building the same function in Airtable made it less intimidating and more accessible.Full Details
    • I built a mobile-friendly way for technicians to reach the SOPs from the field: no lost or crumpled pages, versioning issues, SharePoint access issues, or long searches through dense paragraphs in Word documents.Full Details
  5. Refine through real use

    Some refinements come from the people using it: a reply, a question, or a workaround. Many I catch on my own. I take pride in my work, and I like to chew on the pinch points until things have a proper polish. Each round makes the system a little easier to use.

    In practice

    • Technicians didn't complain about the color-coded follow-up lists. I recognized the colors helped me but could overwhelm them, so I broke each list into separate tables: cleaner and easier to scan, especially for long lists.Full Details
    • A few months after using the onboarding guide, a new hire shared that it was still useful as a reference, and that simplifying it further could make it even more accessible.Full Details

About me

What I notice

I notice the small frictions most people have learned to work around.

The question that gets asked every week. The spreadsheet kept "just in case." The new hire who doesn't want to ask again. I can't leave those alone when I know I can improve the situation. As someone who prides themselves on excellence and efficiency, I understand the hidden costs behind each point of friction. I would rather spend an afternoon making something clear than watch a team lose an hour to confusion every day.

Show more about this

I once showed a very rough draft of my training guide to a dispatcher, and she said, “Man, I wish there had been something like this when I started!” I think a lot of people feel that way. These tool and knowledge gaps live in a strange middle ground. They’re painful, just not painful enough to replace the clunky workaround that’s “good enough.”

Even when friction shows up every day, it rarely gets attention until it becomes an emergency. I have a hard time accepting that, especially when this work comes so naturally to me. I feel a bit like Spider-Man: with these skills comes a responsibility to use them for good, for teams of every shape and size. All I have to do is listen.

How I work

I listen for friction, uncover root causes and nuances, then develop iterative solutions that spark delight and fit within the ecosystem.

I design for the person on the other end of the system, and I keep refining long after something is "good enough," because I take pride in a proper polish. I learn whatever the problem needs, whether that's a database, a macro, or a website, and I'm comfortable using AI tools to get there faster.

Show more about this

The sweetest sound in the world to me is someone saying, “Wow.” I got an “Oh damn!” from the Superintendent when I showed them role-based SOP views and interactive checklists for start-up technicians. Another said, “This looks good. REAL good,” when he saw how I had reworked and expanded the dispatch board.

I like building things people can get excited about: things that go beyond the ask, plan for the future, and account for everything my solutions touch. That’s how I work. It’s not just fixing today’s problem. It’s designing tomorrow’s solution.

What I believe

Confidence is built on clarity and certainty.

When people know what to do and where to find the answer, they are empowered to act faster, make fewer mistakes, and take ownership over their piece of the pie. That's what I bring to a team: curiosity, thoughtfulness, and the tenacity to take an idea from "would be nice" all the way into everyday use.

A Year in Review

Over the past year, I’ve turned scattered notes, long documents, and manual follow-ups into time-saving tools that improve communication every day. New dispatchers find their footing in weeks instead of months, and they answer their own questions without waiting on a busy teammate. Supervisors book crews with confidence, because a holiday conflict surfaces long before it turns into an empty job site and a disappointed customer. Technicians find their next step in seconds from the field, and leaders hear about what’s coming instead of what went wrong. The team feels it as fewer interruptions, fewer surprises, and more time for the work and the customer.
