What I bring to the table

When I build systems, the felt change is an everyday experience of ease when taking action, searching for key details, and tracking progress. It's less talk, more movement, and faster coordination for the whole team. Here are a few examples of how that shows up in real time.

  1. New hires
    People become self-sufficient sooner.

    Guides and checklists introduce workflows through a natural progression with intuitive scaffolding, so new hires build confidence on their own instead of pulling experienced teammates off the job for every question.

  2. The team
    Work doesn't depend on who remembers.

    The process lives in the system, not in one person's head, so a vacation, a promotion, or a departure doesn't stall jobs or customer commitments.

  3. Leaders
    Leaders stop being the switchboard.

    Status, next steps, and answers reach people before they have to ask, so managers spend their time on decisions, customers, and growth instead of chasing updates.

  4. The department
    Change lands instead of piling up.

    When a platform or process shifts, the documentation and tools shift with it, so the team keeps its footing through a rollout.

  5. Over time
    Systems keep getting better.

    I don't hand off and walk away. Each system is watched in use and refined from what people actually do with it, so it improves as the team grows.

The result

Departments can take on more work without being outpaced by coordination load.

How I work

The same five steps sit behind every project.

  1. Listen for friction

    Sometimes things jump out at me, sometimes they're brought to my attention through an indirect comment or an explicit request for help. The signals I listen for:

    • The same question asked twice
    • A side spreadsheet kept "just in case"
    • "Who has the latest version?"
    • Information re-typed from one place to another
    • A task that only works when one person is in

    In practice

    • After I showed another dispatcher my first draft of the training guide, she said, "I wish there were something like this when I started!"Full Details→
    • The Service Manager pulled out 12 pages of paper SOPs with dense paragraphs of text and said, "Our techs don't really read this."Full Details→
    • The Airtable manager dashboard started with a request from the Service Manager to "freshen up" the old New Hire Protocols.Full Details→
  2. Spot the real problem

    When something slips, I look at the system before the person. I identify bottlenecks, redundancies, and communication breakdowns, then ask myself, "What would make the right action the easy one?"

    In practice

    • When a technician flagged incorrect PM parts, I built a monthly audit so each technician sees, and can correct, the parts to be ordered for their sites that month.Full Details→
    • When delayed POs were slowing job closure, I developed a way to request consolidated invoices from vendors, instead of waiting for system flags and in-the-moment delays to signal the need.
  3. Consider use cases and determine ownership

    I look at the end goal, the most efficient way to get there, and decide up front which decisions stay with whom. I simplify information, filter for relevance, and streamline steps for efficiency.

    In practice

    • The original New Hire Protocols named owners for some tasks, but gave no broader view of who is responsible for what. In the new system, every step has one owner, from request to confirmation.Full Details→
    • The original start-up procedures bundled every role's responsibilities into one document. The mobile site gives each role its own view, so people find the right information faster.Full Details→
    • Union holiday conflicts are flagged on the dispatch board, never blocked. The supervisor keeps the final call.Full Details→
  4. Design intuitive solutions with user-friendly interfaces

    I consider existing tools and habits, identify potential break points, and develop tools that improve access without adding complexity.

    In practice: I'm comfortable with VBA and macros, but most people aren't, even when it's just pressing a button. Building the same function in Airtable made it less intimidating and more accessible.

  5. Iterate toward polish

    Every system starts as a first version. Some refinements come from the people using it: a reply, a question, or a workaround. Many I catch on my own. I take pride in my work, and I like to chew on the pinch points until things have a proper polish. Each round makes the system a little easier to use.

    In practice

    • Technicians didn't complain about the color-coded follow-up lists. I recognized the colors helped me but could overwhelm them, so I broke each list into separate tables: cleaner and easier to scan, especially for long lists.Full Details→
    • A few months after using the onboarding guide, a new hire shared that it was still useful as a reference, and that simplifying it further could make it even more accessible.Full Details→

About

I'm Crystal. In Southland's Northern California Service Department, I've turned the way work gets done into systems the team can rely on.

  • A knowledge base that carried dispatch through the move to ServiceTitan
  • Onboarding that takes a new dispatcher from day one to self-sufficient
  • Start-up procedures technicians can pull up on a job site
  • A crew dispatch board that accounts for every technician's union holidays
  • A lifecycle system that runs every new hire, role change, and departure the same way

The felt impact is fewer questions waiting on one person, fewer jobs held up by a missing detail, and less time spent tracking down what's current.
